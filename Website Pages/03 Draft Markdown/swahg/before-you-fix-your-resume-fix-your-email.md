I have heard this sentence from a new learner before.

"I have been applying, but nobody replies. It has been three months."

Usually, the first thing we want to check is the resume. Is the format good? Is the experience enough? Is the title clear?

I can look at the resume.

But before we do that, I want to ask something smaller.

What email address are you using to apply?

Because sometimes, the problem is not the resume. Sometimes the problem is who the resume came from. The resume may be decent, but the email address already made the first impression for you.

And it was not the impression you wanted.

The voice in your head may be saying, "Maybe my resume is not enough. Maybe I need to rebuild everything again. Maybe clients just do not notice me."

I understand why you keep fixing the resume first.

I know that feeling.

That is the visible thing. That is the file you attach. That is the thing you worked hard on.

But remote work has small filters before the big filters. Your email address is one of them.

If a hiring manager sees something like cutiepie_maricel_2003@yahoo.com, they do not need three minutes to decide. They may not even open the file. It sounds harsh, but this is why I teach this early. The email address is a first filter.

Not because clients are perfect.

Not because a Gmail account makes you skilled.

Not because a clean address replaces real work.

But because trust starts before the call.

Here is the simple rule I want every beginner to remember: your freelance email should be boring in the best way.

Use your real name. If your exact name is already taken, add a middle initial, your full last name, or a simple work word. Use Gmail first. Do not add your birth year. Do not use nicknames that belong to a personal chat. Keep one email for freelance work and another one for personal life.

That separation matters.

When a client thread is beside shopping receipts, bank alerts, school reminders, and family messages, your mind gets messy too. I know that sounds small, but the small things become the work environment. If the inbox is confusing, the workday becomes confusing.

This is why I keep separate identities.

One email for work. One email for community and personal brand. One email for personal life.

When a message comes in, I know which hat I need to wear. I am not guessing. I am not searching. I am not mixing client communication with everything else in my life.

That is not overcomplicated. That is order.

And order is a professional skill.

You also need a simple signature. Your name. Your role. Your LinkedIn link, if you have one. Your portfolio link, if you have one. Your city and country. A clean headshot helps too. Not a beach selfie. Not an anime profile. Just a clear face and a plain background.

You do not need a paid domain at the beginning.

That can come later.

Maybe within twelve months, when freelancing becomes more serious, you can move toward yourname.com or a business email. But do not wait for that before you look professional. A clean Gmail account is enough for the first step.

Yes, you dig the soil. But it is the Lord who makes it grow.

That is how I think about small preparation. We cannot force a client to reply. We cannot control every hiring manager. But we can be faithful with the small things we can prepare.

Your email address is one of those small things.

Tonight, before you rewrite your resume again, make one clean freelance Gmail.

Use your real name.

Turn on two-step verification.

Add a plain profile photo.

Create a simple signature.

Then send one test email to yourself and ask, "If I were a client, would I trust this person enough to open the resume?"

Just one clean setup tonight.

That is enough for the next step.

- Lala
