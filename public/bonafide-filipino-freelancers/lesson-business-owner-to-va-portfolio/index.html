From Business Owner to VA Portfolio: Turning Your Shop Into Remote Proof

Back to Top

You already ran the back office

You may think you are starting from zero because you have never held the title Virtual Assistant. But if you ran a business, you already ran a back office. You tracked stock. You chased suppliers. You recorded what came in and what went out. You answered customer messages all day. You followed up on orders. You kept the whole thing moving without anyone reminding you.

That is not nothing. That is the exact work a remote client wants a VA to do. Your next step is not to erase your business history. Your next step is to translate it.

Your shop may not look like a remote client setup. You may have tracked stock in a notebook, recorded sales in a GCash history, or kept supplier numbers in your phone. You may have no clean records you can legally or safely show. That does not mean the work did not happen. It means the proof needs to be rebuilt in tools a remote client recognizes.

A resume tells a client what you did. A VA portfolio shows how you work. A resume line like "ran an online shop" is too vague for a remote client. An inventory tracker with stock levels, reorder points, supplier columns, and a short process note gives the client something they can actually inspect.

O*NET describes secretaries and administrative assistants as workers who perform routine administrative functions such as drafting correspondence, scheduling appointments, organizing files, maintaining databases, and providing information. (O*NET OnLine) Read that list as a business owner and you will see you already did most of it, just for your own shop instead of for a client.

The principle is simple. Do not invent experience. Convert responsibilities into samples. A recreated sample is not dishonest when it is clearly labeled. An anonymized tracker is not fake when you remove private information and rebuild only the structure.

What VA clients actually need proof of

Clients do not hire a VA because she says she is organized. They hire her because they believe she can keep their business from becoming messy. That belief comes from proof: trackers, templates, systems, and clear explanations.

Public VA guidance points to the same task families again and again. Guidance from sites like Remote.co and FlexJobs consistently lists VA skills such as calendar and schedule management, data entry and spreadsheet tracking, email and inbox organization, Google Workspace, online research, CRM support, document preparation, and protecting confidential information.

Look at that list as a former owner. You did inventory data entry. You did supplier communication. You did customer inbox management. You did order tracking. The task families match. The only thing missing is proof a remote client can see.

A weak sample says: "Here is a Google Sheet I made." A strong sample says: "This Google Sheet tracks inventory for a fictional online clothing shop. It includes item, SKU, stock on hand, reorder point, supplier, lead time, and a low-stock flag. I would update it daily and use the summary tab to tell the owner what to reorder." The second version shows judgment. It tells the client what the tracker is for and what problem it solves.

The owner-to-VA translation formula

Use this three-step formula for every portfolio piece. It keeps you honest and specific at the same time.

1. Name the old responsibility.
Example: "I tracked which products were running low and reordered from suppliers."

2. Name the business action inside it.
Example: "I monitored stock levels, set reorder points, coordinated with suppliers, and prevented stockouts."

3. Build a Google Workspace sample that proves it.
Example: "I created an inventory tracker for a fictional online shop with stock levels, reorder points, supplier details, and a low-stock flag."

This keeps you credible. You are not claiming you managed a remote client's warehouse. You are saying: "In my own shop I managed inventory and suppliers. I built this sample to show how I would do the same for a client." That sentence is true and strong.

The most common mistake owners make is shrinking their experience because they were "just" running a small store. Replace small-shop wording with skill language. "I sold stuff online" becomes "I managed product listings, stock levels, customer orders, and supplier coordination for an online retail operation." Same work. Stronger frame.

The proof ladder: three honest rungs

You do not need a real client to build proof. You climb three honest rungs, and every rung is clearly labeled as practice, never as paid client work.

Rung 1: A practice sample. A single recreated artifact, like an inventory tracker built with fictional products. It shows you can build the tool.

Rung 2: A simulated client project. A short case study using fictional data that shows the tool in use. Structure it as: the situation, the concern, the information you reviewed, the asset you built, the follow-up, the update you would send the client, and a confidentiality note. This shows judgment, not just skill.

Rung 3: The portfolio case study. You assemble the samples and the case study into a clean Google Sites page a client can open in one click.

The difference between a recreated sample and a lie is one sentence: a label that says the data is fictional and the work behavior is real. Always include it.

The six Google Workspace samples to build

You do not need all six to start. Build two or three first, then add the rest. Each one maps to work you already did.

Sample 1: Inventory / stock tracker

Why it matters: proves data discipline and the ability to keep numbers a client can trust. Tool: Google Sheets. What to include: item, SKU, stock on hand, reorder point, supplier, lead time, low-stock flag, and a summary tab that says what to reorder. Add a short note: "I would update this daily and flag low stock before it runs out."

Sample 2: Vendor and supplier tracker

Why it matters: proves you can manage outside relationships and deadlines. Tool: Google Sheets. What to include: supplier name (fictional), product, order date, expected date, status, last follow-up, and next action. This is the same skill a client needs for managing their own vendors.

Sample 3: Income and expense tracker

Why it matters: proves recordkeeping, the foundation of bookkeeping VA work. Tool: Google Sheets. What to include: date, description, category, money in, money out, and a running total, all with fictional figures. Read the bookkeeping caution below before you build this one.

Sample 4: Customer reply template library

Why it matters: proves communication and tone, which clients value highly. Tool: Google Docs. What to include: templates for an order confirmation, a shipping update, an out-of-stock reply, a refund or return reply, and a polite follow-up. Show warmth and clarity.

Sample 5: Order and client intake form

Why it matters: proves you can gather the right information cleanly. Tool: Google Forms. What to include: the fields you would need to take an order or onboard a client without back-and-forth. Show that you ask the right questions up front.

Sample 6: A simulated client case study

Why it matters: this is rung two of the proof ladder, and it is what separates a strong portfolio from a folder of templates. Tool: Google Docs. What to include: a short, fictional scenario where a busy shop owner is drowning in stock and supplier chaos, the trackers you would set up, how you would run them, and the weekly update you would send. End with the confidentiality note.

One sample resume bullet per business type

Use these as patterns. Replace the details with your own, but keep the structure: scope, system, and skill, with no invented metrics.

  • Online shop: "Managed product listings, stock levels, customer orders, and supplier coordination for an online retail operation; built inventory and order-tracking systems to keep fulfillment on time."
  • Local retail or sari-sari store: "Ran daily operations for a retail store, including inventory tracking, supplier reordering, cash recording, and customer service."
  • Service business: "Coordinated bookings, client communication, scheduling, and follow-ups for a service business; kept a tracker so no appointment or payment was missed."
  • Family business: "Supported operations of a family business across inventory, supplier follow-up, recordkeeping, and customer messages, keeping the back office organized."

Honesty and confidentiality rules

Every sample carries a label. Use this one: "Recreated portfolio sample. This sample is based on anonymized patterns from prior business experience. All names, products, suppliers, dates, and figures are fictional. No confidential information is included." A short version works too: "Recreated sample using fictional data and anonymized work patterns."

Never include real customer names, phone numbers, or addresses; real supplier names or contracts; real bank, GCash, or payment records; real receipts or invoices; real screenshots of Shopee, Lazada, or Facebook Marketplace dashboards; or real sales figures. Rebuild the structure with fictional data instead. The structure is the skill. The private data is not yours to show.

Do not write "I grew sales by thirty percent" or "I had a hundred thousand pesos in monthly sales" unless it is true, verifiable, and you are willing to back it up. Unverified numbers are the fastest way to lose a client's trust. Show the system, not a sales claim.

The bookkeeping caution every owner needs

Many owners kept records in a notebook, in GCash history, or just in memory. Many mixed personal and business money in one wallet. If that was you, that is normal, and it does not disqualify you. But it does mean two things.

First, do not present informal records as professional bookkeeping you do not actually have. If your real records were loose, do not claim audited books. Build a clean, fictional income and expense tracker to show you can keep records going forward, and say plainly that it is a sample.

Second, be careful before you call yourself a Bookkeeping VA. Bookkeeping has real responsibilities, and a client may expect skills you have not built yet. It is honest and safe to start as a general or admin VA who can keep trackers, and to grow into bookkeeping with proper study. Frame your shop experience as recordkeeping discipline, which is real and valuable, not as formal accounting.

How to present the portfolio

Put your samples in one Google Drive folder, then build a simple Google Sites page with a few sections: a short summary of who you are, your transferable skills, your sample activities with links, a confidentiality note, and a contact section. The companion lesson Google Sites Portfolio walks through the build click by click. Open the sample portfolio at the top of this lesson to see the finished shape first.

Set every shared file to view-only for anyone with the link, so a client can open your work without being able to change it.

FAQ

Can I build a VA portfolio if my business was small or informal? Yes. Size does not matter. The skills, inventory, suppliers, recordkeeping, and customer service, are the same whether the shop was big or small.

Can I use fictional data? Yes, and you should. Recreate the structure of your work with fictional products, suppliers, and figures, and label it clearly. Never use real private data.

Do I need real VA clients first? No. The whole point of this lesson is to build proof before your first client, honestly, through recreated samples.

Should I call myself a Bookkeeping VA? Only if you have the skills. Start as an admin or general VA who keeps clean trackers, and grow into bookkeeping with study. See the bookkeeping caution above.

What should I avoid? Real customer or supplier data, real financial records, real screenshots, unverified sales claims, and any sample that is not labeled as recreated.

Final position

You did not just sell things. You ran inventory, suppliers, records, and customer care, the back office of a real business. A remote client needs exactly that. Translate it, rebuild it as honest samples, and put it where a client can see it. You are not a beginner pretending. You are a Business Operator becoming a Remote Beginner, with proof.
